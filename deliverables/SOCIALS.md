## 1. The hook

The wound does not close until the sponges come home.

We built OR Sentinel at HP Edge AI, SJSU. One box. Eight operating rooms. The nurse says the count out loud. The camera looks at the tray. If those two stories do not match, the close stays locked.

The model does not get a vote. A rule does. SAFE. CAUTION. BLOCK.

A missed sponge is not a vibe. It is a retained item. So we trained a detector on 7,201 composite trays, published the recall (0.817), and then refused to let that score lock the room by itself. The nurse’s count stays official. The chart stays on the box. Synthetic cases. Not a medical device. The hold on the close is the product.

## 2. The numbers, said loud

We did not ask a 27B model “should we close?”

We asked a camera “what is on the tray?” and a rule “do the integers match?”

OR Sentinel, one edge station for eight rooms:

- Detector, 40 epochs, 7,201 trays: recall 0.817, mAP50 0.884
- CountNet: exact on 25 of 32 held-out trays
- The lock itself: 16 safety checks, 16 passed
- A short tray before close is a caution. The same short tray at close is a block.

That last line is the whole hack. Intelligence can misread a sponge. The interlock cannot wave it through.

## 3. Final pitch

Eight rooms. One nurse’s count. Zero charts leaving the building.

OR Sentinel listens, looks, and then waits. Speech tracks sponges in and sponges out. Vision boxes the tray. The only thing allowed to say BLOCK is a fixed rule, and only after someone asks to close.

We ran it on the edge, pointed every model at its own port, and kept the patient record on the box. Acted lines. Composite trays. Not a medical device. The circulating nurse still owns the count. What we changed is the moment everyone wants to rush: the close.
